## 1. Domain Summary

I write **persuasive, honest, tailored proposals for freelance marketplaces** — Upwork, Contra, and Freelancer.com — plus the collateral around them: cover letters, discovery questions, scope of work, fixed-price estimates, hourly responses, and follow-ups.

**The 2026 shift that matters:** marketplaces are now saturated with AI-generated boilerplate, and clients have adapted. The differentiator is no longer polish or speed — it is **evidence of comprehension**. A proposal that proves you read *this* job post and understood *this* business problem beats a beautifully written generic one every time. Clients skim the first 2–3 lines in a crowded inbox; that opening must reference their specific situation, not my résumé.

A prior version of this note researched **enterprise RFP software** (Loopio, Responsive, AutoRFP). That is **off-domain** — those are bid-management platforms for corporate procurement, not freelance-marketplace bidding. This playbook corrects that scope.

**Never:** copy a generic template; exaggerate or invent experience; open with "I am excited to apply"; lead with my own biography; bid on work I cannot deliver; let a proposal sound machine-written.

## 2. Core Workflow

### Phase A — Qualify (before writing a single word)
1. **Read the whole post twice**, including budget, timeline, attachments, and required questions. Missing a required screening question is an instant disqualifier.
2. **Decide whether to bid at all.** Score the fit: do I have real, demonstrable experience? Is the budget serious? Is the scope defined enough to price? A disciplined pass protects both the win-rate and the client relationship.
3. **Read the client's history** — hire rate, average pay, past reviews, verified payment method, whether they have hired for this skill before. This tells me their sophistication level and how to pitch.
4. **Extract the real problem.** The stated ask ("build me a scraper") and the underlying business problem ("I need competitor pricing daily without hiring an analyst") are rarely the same. The proposal must speak to the second.

### Phase B — Plan the angle
5. **Name the single strongest proof point** I have for this exact problem — a comparable project, a measurable outcome, a relevant artifact. One specific, verifiable proof beats a list of five vague ones.
6. **Identify the client's likely risk.** Most freelance clients have been burned before: ghosting, scope creep, missed deadlines, unusable handoff. Decide which risk to neutralise explicitly.
7. **Choose the hook** — the opening line that proves comprehension. It should be something only a person who actually read the post could write.

### Phase C — Draft
8. **Structure (marketplace-optimised, short):**
   - **Hook** — their problem, restated in their words, 1–2 lines.
   - **Solution** — the approach, in plain language, concrete steps.
   - **Proof** — the one relevant example, with an outcome.
   - **Risk-killer** — how I handle the thing they're afraid of (communication cadence, milestones, handoff).
   - **Questions** — 2–3 sharp discovery questions that demonstrate expertise.
   - **CTA** — one clear, low-friction next step.
9. **Keep it scannable.** Short paragraphs, no walls of text, minimal formatting gimmicks. Length should be as short as it can be while still proving competence — typically well under a page.
10. **Write consultatively, not submissively.** Peer-to-peer expert tone. No begging, no "I would be honoured," no superlatives about myself.
11. **Ask questions that only an expert would ask.** Good discovery questions are the cheapest credibility in a proposal — they demonstrate domain fluency and simultaneously de-risk the scope.

### Phase D — Price & scope
12. **Price the outcome, not the hours**, wherever the scope is definable. For fixed-price, state explicit deliverables, milestone boundaries, and what is *excluded*.
13. **State assumptions in writing.** Every fixed-price estimate carries assumptions; unstated ones become disputes.
14. **Never quote a number I cannot defend.** If scope is genuinely unclear, propose a small paid discovery milestone rather than guessing.

### Phase E — Human-voice pass (mandatory)
15. **Strip AI tells:** "I am excited to," "delve," "seamless," "leverage," "in today's fast-paced world," triads of adjectives, em-dash-heavy cadence, and uniformly identical sentence lengths.
16. **Read it aloud.** If it doesn't sound like a competent human talking to another human, rewrite it.
17. **Verify every factual claim about my experience.** Honesty is a hard rule — no inflated timelines, no borrowed case studies, no implied clients I never had.
18. **Check compliance:** required questions answered, attachments referenced, platform limits respected.

### Phase F — Follow up & persist
19. **Follow up once, with new value** — a relevant thought, a quick observation about their problem — not "just checking in."
20. **Log the outcome** (sent / viewed / replied / hired / passed) so the win-rate and the angles that work become measurable over time.
21. **Write the note to the Obsidian Vault** at `C:\Users\black\Documents\Obsidian Vault`, then **re-read the file to confirm the write landed** (verify-after-write).
22. **Persist durable facts to Mnemosyne** — client preferences, winning angles, rate outcomes.

## 4. Current Best Practices (2025–2026)

- **Comprehension beats polish.** With AI-written proposals now the default noise floor, the proposals that convert are the ones demonstrating specific understanding of the client's post and business.
- **The first two lines are the whole game.** Clients triage in a preview pane. Front-load the client's problem; never open with your own name, excitement, or credentials.
- **Specificity is the anti-AI signal.** Named tools, real constraints, a concrete number, a real observation about their product — these cannot be faked by a generic generator.
- **Short and dense wins.** Long proposals correlate with low comprehension, not high effort.
- **Discovery questions are a conversion tool**, not a formality — they shift the frame from "applicant" to "consultant."
- **Bid selectively.** On Connects-based platforms, every bid has a real cost; spraying low-fit proposals degrades both economics and win-rate. Fewer, sharper, better-targeted.
- **De-risk explicitly.** Naming the client's likely fear (ghosting, scope creep, poor communication) and answering it pre-emptively is a strong differentiator most bidders skip.
- **Price outcomes with explicit exclusions.** Ambiguity in a fixed-price scope is where margin and goodwill both die.
- **One value-adding follow-up**, then stop. Persistence without new information reads as desperation.
- **Honesty is strategically optimal, not just ethical.** Overstated experience produces mismatched projects, bad reviews, and profile damage that outlives any single win.
- **Track win-rate by angle.** Proposal writing is empirical; without outcome logging, every bid is a guess.

## 5. Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Fix |
|---|---|
| **Snippet-dumping / off-domain research** — the prior version of this note catalogued enterprise RFP software | Scope research to the actual domain: freelance-marketplace bidding. |
| Generic template reuse | Customize every proposal; templates are for structure only, never prose. |
| Opening with "I'm excited to apply" or your own bio | Open with the client's problem, in their words. |
| Ignoring required screening questions | Compliance check before send — an unanswered question is an auto-reject. |
| Walls of text | Short paragraphs, scannable structure, ruthless cutting. |
| Listing every skill you have | One relevant, provable proof point beats a résumé dump. |
| Exaggerated or borrowed experience | Hard rule: never. Verify every claim before sending. |
| Obvious AI cadence ("delve," "seamless," "in today's landscape") | Mandatory human-voice pass; read aloud. |
| Quoting a price with undefined scope | Define exclusions and assumptions, or propose a paid discovery milestone. |
| Bidding on everything | Qualify first; a disciplined pass is a valid outcome. |
| "Just checking in" follow-ups | Follow up once, with new value. |
| Never logging outcomes | Log every send and result; otherwise win-rate can't improve. |
| Writing the note and never re-reading it | Verify-after-write is mandatory. |

Do not rely solely on cached SOP/Playbook knowledge.
- **Tool:** the CRW crawler (`crw_scrape` for pages, `crw_map` for site discovery). Use `crw_map` on the vendor/authority domain, then `crw_scrape` the most relevant pages. Fallback: Jina via shell redirection (see Web Access standard).
- **Depth:** follow the project's key entities (vendor docs, official changelogs, authoritative guides) — not a single query. Capture what changed vs the cached SOP.
- **When NOT required:** pure planning/archival deliverables where the SOP is already authoritative and the user explicitly scopes to vault-only (e.g. Knowledge Sync dry-runs).
- **Record:** note the sources checked in the deliverable (Sources section) and store a Mnemosyne memory of any material update.

### Skill improvements adopted

**1. Split every brief into "instructions" vs "evaluation criteria" before drafting (from FAR Part 15).**
The federal Uniform Contract Format deliberately separates **Section L — instructions, conditions, and notices to offerors** from **Section M — evaluation factors for award**. Buyers state *how to submit* and *how you will be scored* as two different things. Scaled down to marketplace work: on every job post, build a two-column compliance matrix — left column = every instruction and required question (format, attachments, screening questions), right column = every stated or implied criterion the client will judge on. Draft against the right column; verify against the left before send. This upgrades the Phase A read-twice step and the Phase E compliance check from "don't miss anything" into an auditable checklist.

**2. Give every priced bid a defensible price header, and refresh assumptions up to the moment of agreement (from FAR Table 15-2).**
FAR requires the first page of a pricing proposal to carry identifying data plus proposed cost, profit/fee and total up front — and obliges the offeror to keep submitting updated data *up to the time of agreement on price*, not merely at submission. Marketplace adaptation: lead the price section with a one-block header (deliverables, total, explicit exclusions, "priced on assumptions as of DATE"), and if anything material changes during the client conversation, **re-state the assumption before agreeing**, never after. This closes the most common fixed-price dispute vector already flagged in §5.

**3. Branch the opening on solicited vs unsolicited (from Proposify).**
Proposify draws the distinction that a *solicited* proposal answers a request the buyer already issued, while an *unsolicited* one arrives before the buyer has framed a need. These demand different first lines. For a solicited bid (a job post), the hook must prove comprehension of the *stated* ask. For unsolicited or direct outreach, the hook must first establish that the problem exists and is worth money — comprehension alone is insufficient, because nothing has been asked yet. Apply this branch at Phase B step 7 instead of using a single hook formula for both.

**Supporting note (Upwork):** Upwork's own guidance stresses a specific searchable title, a client-focused overview, and portfolio samples matched to the work sought. Consequence for bidding: the profile is read *alongside* the proposal, so before sending, confirm the visible headline and top portfolio piece corroborate the exact proof point the bid claims. A mismatch quietly undercuts an otherwise strong proposal.

### Skill improvements adopted

**1. Nine-section proposal structure (from Proposify, verified live)**
Adopt the canonical section order: Cover → Executive Summary → Problem Statement → Proposed Solution → Deliverables → Milestones → Budget/Pricing → About/Team → Social Proof. The executive summary is **not a summary** — it is a sales pitch with five required components (Opener, Need, Solution, Evidence, CTA). Pricing section: 97.6% of winning proposals include it; interactive tables close 12.6% higher; label "Your Investment" not "Cost."

**2. Solicited vs Unsolicited opening branch (from Proposify)**
For solicited bids (job posts), the hook proves comprehension of the *stated* ask. For unsolicited/cold outreach, the hook must first establish the problem exists and is worth money — comprehension alone is insufficient because nothing has been asked yet. Apply this branch at Phase B step 7.

**3. Executive summary written last (from Proposify)**
Write the executive summary **after** the proposal body — you know the solution better. The five components are non-negotiable: Opener (them, not you), Need (demonstrate grasp + research), Solution (high-level but specific), Evidence (niche experience/unique skill/process/results, brief), CTA (flattery + partnership framing).

**4. SOW best practice: Performance-based preferred (from ProjectManager.com & Institute of PM)**
Three SOW types: Design/detail (buyer directs process), Level of effort/T&M/unit rate (short-term), **Performance-based (preferred — focuses on outcomes, grants autonomy on process)**. Required elements: intro, purpose, scope, WBS (tasks/milestones/deliverables), schedule, requirements & acceptance criteria, payment terms. Get signed off by authorized parties before execution.

**5. Platform-specific mechanics internalized**
- **Upwork:** Profile read alongside proposal — headline + top portfolio must corroborate the bid's proof point. Specific searchable title, client-focused overview, matched portfolio samples.
- **Contra:** Commission-free; portfolio-led; job feed filterable by tools/skills/budgets; native case-study builder; discovery score = visibility gate.
- **Freelancer.com:** Milestone payment protection; Verified badge for high-value; contests alternative to bidding; Preferred Freelancer Program.

**6. FAR Part 15 compliance matrix adapted for marketplace**
Separate **instructions (Section L)** from **evaluation criteria (Section M)** into a two-column compliance matrix per job post. Left column = every instruction/required question/format/page limit; right column = every stated/implied criterion the client will judge on. Draft against right; verify against left before send.

**7. Defensible price header with dated assumptions**
Every fixed-price bid leads with a one-block header: deliverables, total, explicit exclusions, "priced on assumptions as of DATE." If anything material changes during conversation, **re-state the assumption before agreeing**, never after.

**8. Competitive differentiation via single proof + risk neutralization**
One specific, verifiable proof point beats five vague claims. Explicitly name the client's likely risk (ghosting, scope creep, missed deadlines, poor handoff) and neutralize it. Discovery questions = cheapest credibility — ask what only an expert would ask. Case studies are the tipping point; treat as essential selling tools, not optional.

**9. Proposal software for tracking + CRM integration**
Proposify/PandaDoc track: opens, reads, time per section, stuck points, forwards to decision-makers. Integrate with CRMs (Salesforce, HubSpot, Pipedrive) + Zapier. Automate: template population, approval workflows (by deal size/discount), client input forms. SSO with Salesforce/Okta/Azure AD.

**10. Follow-up: one value-add only, then stop; outcome logging mandatory**
One follow-up with new value (relevant thought/observation on their problem). Never "just checking in." Log every outcome (sent/viewed/replied/hired/passed) to measure win-rate by angle.

**11. Human-voice pass mandatory (reaffirmed)**
Strip AI tells: "excited to," "delve," "seamless," "leverage," "in today's fast-paced world," triad adjectives, em-dash cadence, uniform sentence lengths. Read aloud — must sound like competent human to competent human.
